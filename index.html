02 · Historical Comparisons

Historical Data

Job Creation

Democratic vs Republican Administrations

What this shows

Since 1945, Democratic presidents have presided over 83 million total jobs — more than 2.5 times the 32 million created under Republican administrations. On a monthly basis that's 164,000 new jobs per month under Democrats vs just 61,000 under Republicans. Even when adjusting for the number of years each party held office, Democrats have outpaced Republicans at every time horizon measured.

GDP Performance

Average Annual GDP Growth by Study

What this shows

Four completely independent studies — Princeton's Blinder & Watson, CNN's analysis, the NY Times, and a 2024 presidential data review — all reach the same conclusion: the US economy grows roughly 1.7 percentage points faster per year under Democratic presidents. That gap compounds over a four-year term into an economy that is approximately 7% larger than it would have been under average Republican stewardship. The consistency across studies makes this one of the most replicated findings in presidential economics.

Unemployment Trends

Unemployment Rate Changes

What this shows

Under Democratic administrations, unemployment has averaged 5.2% and typically falls during their tenure — dropping an average of 0.8 percentage points by the end of a term. Under Republican administrations, unemployment averages a higher 5.8% and tends to rise — increasing by 1.1 percentage points on average. In other words, Americans are more likely to be working when a Democrat is in office, and more likely to lose work when a Republican is.

Workforce Concentration

Immigrant Share of U.S. Workforce by Sector

What this shows

Immigrants make up 18% of the total U.S. workforce overall — but their share in critical sectors is far higher. 61% of all U.S. farmworkers are immigrants, including 36–57% who are undocumented. More than a third of hospitality and food service workers in major metros are immigrants. Nearly a quarter of construction workers are immigrants. The Trump administration's own Department of Labor acknowledged in October 2025 that the near-total end of undocumented worker inflow poses "immediate dangers to the American food supply" because American workers are "unwilling or unable" to take agricultural positions. Healthcare System Exposure

Foreign-Born Workers in U.S. Healthcare

What this shows

The U.S. healthcare system — already strained by chronic worker shortages — is disproportionately staffed by foreign-born workers at every level. 1 in 4 physicians and surgeons (26%) are foreign-born, as are 27.5% of registered nurses. In rural areas, where shortages are most acute, immigrants fill an even larger share of roles. The KFF notes that reducing immigration "could reduce access to care, lead to understaffing and poorer quality of care, and increase hospitals' labor costs" — costs that get passed to patients. Peterson Institute Modeling

Deportation Scale vs Economic Damage by 2028

What this shows

The Peterson Institute modeled two deportation scenarios. Even a moderate deportation of 1.3 million people would shrink GDP by 1.2%, reduce employment by 1.1%, and raise prices by 1.5% by 2028. Scaling to 8.3 million deportations — closer to what the current administration has signaled — would cause a 7.4% GDP reduction, meaning the U.S. economy would effectively produce no net growth at all during Trump's second term, with a 9.1% spike in consumer prices on top. The American Immigration Council separately estimated a sustained deportation campaign would cost between $1.1 and $1.7 trillion in lost GDP. JEC Source →

Yale Budget Lab · Distributional Impact

Who Pays the Most — Tariff Burden by Income Level

What this shows

Tariffs are a regressive tax — meaning they hit lower-income families hardest as a share of their budget. A family in the bottom income quintile loses roughly 4–4.5% of their income to tariff-driven price increases, while a top-1% household loses less than 0.7%. This is because low-income families spend a larger portion of their money on goods (food, clothing, household items) and less on services. The tariff is the same rate for everyone — but it takes proportionally three to four times more from a working-class family than from a wealthy one. Yale Budget Lab →

BLS / EPI · Real Wage Erosion

When Inflation Overtook Wages — 2022 to 2026

What this shows

From 2023 to 2024, wages were winning — growing faster than inflation and giving workers a real raise. That reversed in late 2025 when inflation crossed above wage growth for the first time in two years. Inflation is now running at 3.8% while wages grow at just 3.6% — a 0.2-point gap that quietly erases $120 per year from a $60,000 household budget. For low-wage workers in retail, food service, and home care, real wages actually went backward in 2025 — the first reversal in five years. EPI →  USAFacts →
