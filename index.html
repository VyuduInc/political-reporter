02 · Modern Era Scorecard

Democrats vs Republicans

A president-by-president breakdown of every major economic metric in the modern era. Each number is sourced directly from BLS, BEA, Gallup, and the University of Michigan.

Net Jobs per Presidency

Jobs Added by President — Modern Era

What this shows

This is the starkest number in presidential economics. Democratic presidents in the modern era added a combined +49.9 million net jobs — Clinton +22.7M, Obama +11.6M, Biden +15.6M. Republican presidents netted just −0.82 million — Bush Jr +1.3M, Trump's first term −2.7M, Trump's second term +0.58M so far. That's a difference of over 50 million jobs across the same 33-year period. The bars below zero represent net job destruction — something that has only happened under Republican administrations in this era.

Gallup Presidential Approval

Average Approval Rating by President

What this shows

Democratic presidents averaged 48.0% approval vs 44.1% for Republicans across the modern era — a 3.9-point gap. Clinton's 55.1% remains the highest of the six presidencies shown. Both Trump terms average in the low 40s, the lowest of any modern president. Approval ratings reflect how Americans actually felt living under each president's economy — not just the data on paper.

UMich Sentiment

Consumer Sentiment by President

What this shows

Clinton-era consumers were the most confident in modern history (~99). The most striking outlier is Trump's first term (~96) — Americans actually felt good before COVID hit. Trump's second term (~50) is the opposite extreme: the lowest reading in 74 years, suggesting the economic anxiety from tariffs and layoffs is hitting household psychology hard.

Gallup Economic Confidence

First-Year Economic Confidence

What this shows

In their first year in office, Democratic presidents averaged 61% economic confidence vs 47% for Republicans — a 14-point gap. Clinton and Obama both entered with the public broadly optimistic about the economy's direction. Every Republican first year sits below 50%, with Trump's second term (44%) the lowest of all six.

Post-WW2 Recession Frequency

10/11

Recessions that began under Republican presidents

Of the 11 recessions recorded since World War II, 10 began under Republican administrations. The single exception was a brief 1945 contraction under Truman as WWII spending wound down. This pattern holds across every major economic study of the post-war era — including the Princeton Blinder & Watson study and JEC analysis.

The One Republican Edge — And Why It Disappears

2.6%

Rep avg
vs 3.0% Dem

Inflation is the only metric where Republicans hold a slight modern-era edge — 2.6% average vs 3.0% for Democrats. But that edge is entirely driven by Biden's post-COVID spike (which hit every developed economy). Remove Biden's term and Democrats' inflation average drops below Republicans'. More importantly, Trump's second term is currently running at 3.8% — above both party averages — erasing the Republican advantage entirely.

02 · Historical Comparisons

Historical Data

Job Creation

Democratic vs Republican Administrations

What this shows

Since 1945, Democratic presidents have presided over 83 million total jobs — more than 2.5 times the 32 million created under Republican administrations. On a monthly basis that's 164,000 new jobs per month under Democrats vs just 61,000 under Republicans. Even when adjusting for the number of years each party held office, Democrats have outpaced Republicans at every time horizon measured.

GDP Performance

Average Annual GDP Growth by Study

What this shows

Four completely independent studies — Princeton's Blinder & Watson, CNN's analysis, the NY Times, and a 2024 presidential data review — all reach the same conclusion: the US economy grows roughly 1.7 percentage points faster per year under Democratic presidents. That gap compounds over a four-year term into an economy that is approximately 7% larger than it would have been under average Republican stewardship. The consistency across studies makes this one of the most replicated findings in presidential economics.

Unemployment Trends

Unemployment Rate Changes

What this shows

Under Democratic administrations, unemployment has averaged 5.2% and typically falls during their tenure — dropping an average of 0.8 percentage points by the end of a term. Under Republican administrations, unemployment averages a higher 5.8% and tends to rise — increasing by 1.1 percentage points on average. In other words, Americans are more likely to be working when a Democrat is in office, and more likely to lose work when a Republican is.

Monthly Trend

Job Creation 2025–2026

What this shows

Monthly job creation collapsed at the start of Trump's second term, falling from the 125,000/month pace of 2024 to as low as 8,000 jobs in July 2025 — a level not seen since the COVID shutdowns. The economy added only 584,000 total jobs in 2025, the slowest annual pace since 2020, and that number was subsequently revised down further to 181,000 after BLS benchmark corrections. While monthly figures edged up into early 2026, the pace still falls well short of the ~100,000 needed each month just to absorb new workers entering the labor force.

Immigration & the Economy

What Losing
Immigrants Costs

Based on peer-reviewed research (CU Boulder / NBER, May 2026), Peterson Institute modeling, and current BLS data. Sources linked in the Citations section.

Key Finding — CU Boulder / NBER, May 2026

Mass deportation does not free up jobs for American workers — it eliminates jobs for them. Economist Chloe East's landmark study found no evidence that ICE enforcement benefits U.S.-born workers. If anything, job opportunities for U.S.-born workers go down as a result of enforcement surges. CU Boulder →

Workforce Concentration

Immigrant Share of U.S. Workforce by Sector

What this shows

Immigrants make up 18% of the total U.S. workforce overall — but their share in critical sectors is far higher. 61% of all U.S. farmworkers are immigrants, including 36–57% who are undocumented. More than a third of hospitality and food service workers in major metros are immigrants. Nearly a quarter of construction workers are immigrants. The Trump administration's own Department of Labor acknowledged in October 2025 that the near-total end of undocumented worker inflow poses "immediate dangers to the American food supply" because American workers are "unwilling or unable" to take agricultural positions. Migration Policy Institute →

Healthcare System Exposure

Foreign-Born Workers in U.S. Healthcare

What this shows

The U.S. healthcare system — already strained by chronic worker shortages — is disproportionately staffed by foreign-born workers at every level. 1 in 4 physicians and surgeons (26%) are foreign-born, as are 27.5% of registered nurses. In rural areas, where shortages are most acute, immigrants fill an even larger share of roles. The KFF notes that reducing immigration "could reduce access to care, lead to understaffing and poorer quality of care, and increase hospitals' labor costs" — costs that get passed to patients. Sources: NFAP 2025, KFF, Baker Institute

Peterson Institute Modeling

Deportation Scale vs Economic Damage by 2028

What this shows

The Peterson Institute modeled two deportation scenarios. Even a moderate deportation of 1.3 million people would shrink GDP by 1.2%, reduce employment by 1.1%, and raise prices by 1.5% by 2028. Scaling to 8.3 million deportations — closer to what the current administration has signaled — would cause a 7.4% GDP reduction, meaning the U.S. economy would effectively produce no net growth at all during Trump's second term, with a 9.1% spike in consumer prices on top. The American Immigration Council separately estimated a sustained deportation campaign would cost between $1.1 and $1.7 trillion in lost GDP. JEC Source →

The Chilling Effect — CU Boulder / NBER 2026

1,200 Arrests →
8,774 Workers
Stop Working

When ICE arrests 1,200 people, approximately 7,574 additional undocumented immigrants who weren't arrested still stop showing up to work out of fear — plus 1,200 U.S.-born workers lose their jobs because the businesses they work for can no longer operate at scale. That means for every 6 undocumented workers removed, 1 American worker loses their job too. NPR / Planet Money →

Fiscal Policy

Deficit Comparison

What this shows

Both presidents dramatically expanded the federal deficit. Bush's deficit peaked during the 2008 financial crisis. Trump's first-term deficit exploded to 15.2% of GDP in 2020 — the largest peacetime deficit in US history — due to pandemic relief spending. In Trump's second term, the deficit remains elevated at an estimated 6.5% of GDP in 2025, nearly double the pre-pandemic historical average of roughly 3%, driven by extended tax cuts and reduced federal revenue.

08 · Obama vs Trump

Obama · Trump
Comparison

Job Growth

Cumulative Job Creation

What this shows

Obama inherited the worst job market since the Great Depression — his first year saw a net loss of 3.5 million jobs as the financial crisis continued. But the trajectory steadily improved, finishing with 11.6 million net new jobs over eight years. Trump's first term started strong, adding nearly 5 million jobs in three years, then lost it all — and more — in 2020 due to COVID, ending with a net loss of 2.7 million. The divergence makes clear that Obama's recovery, while slow to start, was far more durable than Trump's pre-pandemic gains.

Presidential Economic Performance 1993–2026

What this shows

This 33-year view traces GDP growth and unemployment together. The Clinton-era boom of the late 1990s produced 4–5% GDP growth with falling unemployment. The 2008–09 recession under Bush caused the deepest GDP collapse since the 1930s and sent unemployment to 9.3%. Obama's recovery was steady but gradual — GDP growth never spectacular but unemployment fell consistently for seven years. The post-COVID rebound in 2021 produced a one-time 5.9% GDP spike, but by 2025 growth had slowed back to 2.1% while unemployment began ticking up again.

Multi-Dimensional

Performance Comparison Radar

What this shows

This spider chart scores three administrations across six economic dimensions simultaneously. A larger filled area means stronger overall performance. Obama's presidency scores highest across nearly every category — consistent job growth, wage gains, and rising consumer confidence. Trump's first term (pre-COVID) performed moderately, strongest on GDP growth. Trump's second term (2025–) shows the smallest footprint of the three — particularly weak on consumer confidence (record lows) and wage growth (declined in real terms during 2025), signaling that most Americans are not feeling economic improvement despite official claims.
